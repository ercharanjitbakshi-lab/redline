# Agent 2 Research: Recurring Contract Clauses That Actually Burn People

Scope: Which clause types show up most in real consumer/tenant/freelancer/worker complaints and discussions, ranked by frequency of complaint evidence, with plain-English definitions, a documented harm, and a source URL for every claim.

Method note: Findings are ranked by how often the clause type surfaces in complaint data, consumer-agency alerts, small-claims patterns, and practitioner write-ups. "Frequency evidence" describes the strength of that signal. Direct quotes are drawn from the sources listed.

---

## 1. Auto-renewal / negative-option billing

**Plain English:** The contract renews itself and keeps charging your card at the end of each term unless you take a specific action to cancel. "Negative option" = silence is treated as a yes. Common in gyms, SaaS, streaming, subscription boxes, software, professional memberships.

**How it burns people:** Consumers forget the renewal date, miss a narrow cancellation window, discover cancellation requires a phone call or in-person visit, or keep getting billed after they thought they cancelled. Often paired with an undisclosed price increase at renewal.

**Documented harm / quote:**
- "Receiving charges for services you have not explicitly opted out of is the number one complaint from subscription-based service customers... Additional complaint types include cancellation fees and difficulty cancelling due to needlessly complex processes, such as requiring a customer place a phone call rather than cancel their subscription online." — LawInfo, consumer-protection explainer. https://www.lawinfo.com/resources/consumer-protection/navigating-the-legalities-of-subscription-services-and-automatic-renewals.html
- The FTC sued Uber under the FTC Act and ROSCA over its subscription (Uber One) billing and cancellation practices — a federal enforcement action specifically about auto-renewal / cancellation friction. https://consumerrescue.org/news-and-alerts/how-protect-against-automatic-subscription-renewals/
- Gym memberships are a canonical case: New York's Division of Consumer Protection issued a dedicated consumer alert on health-club auto-renewal and cancellation traps, and 40+ states have special health-club contract laws. https://dos.ny.gov/news/consumer-alert-nys-department-states-division-consumer-protection-offers-tips-avoid-costly ; https://www.findlaw.com/litigation/filing-a-lawsuit/can-i-sue-my-gym-membership.html

**Frequency evidence:** Strong. Cited as the "number one complaint" category for subscription services (LawInfo); driver of a distinct body of class-action litigation over auto-renewal fees (https://mccunelawgroup.com/class-action-lawyer/subscription-auto-renewal-fees/); subject of a federal rule (ROSCA / FTC "Click to Cancel" negative-option rulemaking) and 40+ state health-club statutes; recent FTC enforcement (Uber).

## 2. Forced arbitration + class-action waiver

**Plain English:** You give up the right to sue in court or join a class action. Disputes go to a private arbitrator, one customer at a time, often under strict notice rules and short deadlines. Standard in credit-card, cell-phone, bank, online-retailer, social-media, rideshare, and warranty terms.

**How it burns people:** When a company overcharges or harms many people by small amounts, individual arbitration makes recovery economically impossible, so misconduct goes unchallenged.

**Documented harm / quote:**
- "If a company steals $50 from a million customers, without class action waiver, lawyers can aggregate claims, making a $50 million case viable, but with class action waiver, each customer must individually pursue $50 — making justice economically impossible." — Wolf Popper LLP. https://www.wolfpopper.com/news/gone-too-far-forced-arbitration-in-consumer-contracts
- Real cases where consumers were pushed out of court: *Gutierrez v. Wells Fargo* (overdraft-fee customers forced to arbitrate); *Flores v. Uber Techs.* (2018 data breach exposing ~57 million riders and 600,000 drivers — victims forced into individual arbitration); *Safadi v. Citibank* (frequent-flyer-miles misreporting class claim dismissed); payday-lender cases *Henry v. Cash Biz* and *McKenzie Check Advance v. Betts*. — Center for Justice & Democracy fact sheet. https://centerjd.org/content/fact-sheet-cases-tossed-out-court-because-forced-arbitration-causes-and-class-action-bans

**Frequency evidence:** Strong on ubiquity. "Arbitration agreements can be found in contractual terms of service for credit cards, cell phones, online retailers, social media platforms, and ridesharing apps, as well as in warranty terms printed on product packaging." https://centerjd.org/content/fact-sheet-cases-tossed-out-court-because-forced-arbitration-causes-and-class-action-bans ; see also https://www.impactfund.org/legal-practitioner-blog/toothless-rights

## 3. Security deposit / repair & cleaning deduction clauses (residential leases)

**Plain English:** Lease language letting the landlord deduct from your deposit for "damage," "cleaning," "repainting," or "excessive wear," and set the terms/timeline for return.

**How it burns people:** Landlords charge tenants for ordinary wear and tear, prior tenants' damage, or routine repainting; or simply return the deposit late or never with no itemization.

**Documented harm / quote:**
- "The landlord should not charge the tenant for ordinary wear and tear... Carpet that is matted is normal wear and tear. Burns or stains on a carpet is damage caused by negligence... You should not be held responsible for damages caused by previous tenants." — Equal Housing renters' rights guide. https://renters.equalhousing.org/repairs-security-deposit/getting-your-security-deposit-back/
- "Disagreements about whether a landlord properly withheld all or part of a tenant's security deposit account for a large percentage of the landlord-tenant disputes that end up in small claims court." — Nolo, *Small Claims Court* guide. https://www.nolo.com/legal-encyclopedia/free-books/small-claims-book/chapter20-2.html

**Frequency evidence:** Strong for the lease context. Security-deposit disputes are repeatedly described as among the most litigated landlord-tenant matters in small claims court (Nolo, above; https://managecasa.com/articles/top-10-common-disputes-between-tenants-and-rental-owners). State AGs run dedicated intake for it (e.g., NY AG rent-security complaint form: https://ag.ny.gov/sites/default/files/nyc-rent-security-complaint-english.pdf). Did not find a single national percentage figure — see gaps.

## 4. Freelance payment-timing clauses: "payment on approval," net-60/90, and kill fees

**Plain English:** Clauses that make payment contingent on the client's subjective "approval" or "acceptance," push payment far out (net-60/90), or replace a full fee with a small "kill fee" if the client cancels a commissioned piece.

**How it burns people:** Clients withhold payment claiming the work wasn't "approved," delay for months, or invoke a kill fee after the freelancer has done most of the work. Freelancers rarely sue because it isn't worth the cost.

**Documented harm / quote:**
- Freelancers Union / partner survey (2022, NY): "62% of freelance workers... had lost wages at least once"; "91%... reported experiencing late or overdue wages"; "54% reporting experiencing delays of 3 months or longer"; "less than one percent of respondents sought a legal remedy." https://blog.freelancersunion.org/2022/05/12/over-60-of-ny-freelancers-report-not-being-paid-for-work-performed/
- Earlier national survey: "50% of freelancers reported trouble collecting payment in 2014, and 71% cite trouble collecting payment at some point in their career," average loss $5,968 in 2014. https://blog.freelancersunion.org/2015/12/10/costs-nonpayment/ ; full report: https://www.onlabor.org/wp-content/uploads/2017/05/FU_NonpaymentReport_r3.pdf
- Freelancer accounts of the mechanism: a client "withheld payment for 3 months, claiming supervisor hadn't approved the blog strategy" ("he owed me around $2,400"); another client, when "it came time for the payment, suddenly the client was backpedaling." https://contently.com/strategist/2016/04/11/5-freelancers-share-worst-client-horror-stories

**Frequency evidence:** Strong. Majority-of-freelancers numbers from repeated Freelancers Union surveys; drove "Freelance Isn't Free" laws in NY and other jurisdictions.

## 5. IP assignment / rights-grab clauses (freelance & creative work)

**Plain English:** "All work product, and all intellectual property rights therein, are hereby assigned to Client." Often written to sweep in drafts, the freelancer's pre-existing tools and templates, and to bar the freelancer from showing the work in a portfolio — sometimes with assignment triggered on delivery rather than on payment.

**How it burns people:** The freelancer loses the right to reuse their own methods and templates, can't show the work to win new clients, and — if pre-existing IP wasn't carved out — risks an infringement claim for reusing their own earlier work. If assignment isn't tied to payment, an unpaid freelancer has already given away the rights.

**Documented harm / quote:**
- Red flags: "Overly broad assignment clauses that transfer all rights to the client, including preliminary drafts, tools, or unrelated work"; "Prohibiting you from showcasing the work in your portfolio or case studies"; "assignment of rights before payment." Consequence: "Freelancers may lose rights to their portfolio, tools, or techniques developed before the project and risk being sued for IP infringement if they reuse their own work elsewhere." — flag.red contract guide. https://flag.red/contract-guides/ip-assignment-in-freelance-agreement

**Frequency evidence:** Moderate. Recurring theme across freelance-contract guides and freelancer discussion; treated as a standard "red flag" checklist item. I did not find a quantified survey on how often IP clauses specifically cause disputes — see gaps.

## 6. Non-compete clauses

**Plain English:** After you leave, you may not work for a competitor, start a competing business, or sometimes work in your field at all, for a set time and geographic area.

**How it burns people:** Blocks workers — including low-wage and mid-income workers with no trade secrets — from taking better jobs or starting their own business, and exposes them to litigation threats from a former employer.

**Documented harm / quote:**
- "Between 36 million and 60 million private-sector workers are subject to noncompete agreements." "13.3 percent of workers earning less than $40,000 annually had a noncompete agreement." Workers report non-competes "blocked them from taking better jobs or starting their own businesses, and subjected them to threats and litigation from their employers." — GAO report (GAO-23-103785). https://www.gao.gov/assets/gao-23-103785.pdf
- FTC: a ban "could increase wages by nearly $300 billion per year" and expand opportunities for "about 30 million Americans." https://www.ftc.gov/news-events/news/press-releases/2023/01/ftc-proposes-rule-ban-noncompete-clauses-which-hurt-workers-harm-competition
- Background and worker stories: EPI, "Noncompete agreements: Ubiquitous, harmful to wages and to competition." https://www.epi.org/publication/noncompete-agreements/ ; FTC final Non-Compete Clause Rule (later challenged in court): https://www.federalregister.gov/documents/2024/05/07/2024-09171/non-compete-clause-rule

**Frequency evidence:** Strong quantitatively — tens of millions of workers; multiple independent surveys cited by GAO, FTC, and EPI.

## 7. Unilateral termination / "termination for convenience"

**Plain English:** One party (usually the one paying) can end the contract "at any time... for convenience and without cause," while the other party has no matching right. Standard in construction (AIA A201), government contracts, and increasingly private-sector service and vendor agreements.

**How it burns people:** The contractor is paid only for work already done plus wind-down costs — not the profit they were counting on for the rest of the job. It can be weaponized: cancel near completion "to avoid making final payment or in an attempt to keep retainage."

**Documented harm / quote:**
- "The owner may, at any time, terminate the Contract for the owner's convenience and without cause." "Lost Profits Aren't Available When a Contract is Terminated for Convenience." "The party in power (the party with the money) will often dictate the terms." — Levelset. https://www.levelset.com/blog/termination-for-convenience/
- "The primary purpose of termination for convenience clauses is to allow the owner to avoid the duty to pay the contractor the profit that the contractor would have earned on the balance of the contract." — Contractor Magazine. https://www.contractormag.com/management/article/20878107/dealing-with-termination-for-convenience-clauses

**Frequency evidence:** Moderate. Described as "really common in government construction contracts" and "increasingly common in the private sector" (Levelset), but I found practitioner commentary rather than complaint-volume data. See also https://www.consensusdocs.org/news/termination-for-convenience-clauses-maybe-more-than-just-convenience/

## 8. Indemnification / "hold harmless" clauses

**Plain English:** You agree to cover the other party's losses, legal defense costs, and settlements arising from the deal — sometimes "regardless of cause," meaning even when their own negligence caused the problem.

**How it burns people:** Shifts liability onto the smaller party. A single lawsuit against the larger party becomes the smaller party's bill for defense and settlement. Business liability insurance often excludes contractual indemnification, so it isn't covered.

**Documented harm / quote:**
- "Broad indemnification clauses can shift massive liability to small businesses, potentially leaving you paying for someone else's mistakes. It only takes one bad paragraph in one contract to wrap a company up in multi-million dollar litigation." "Insurance doesn't always save you, as many liability policies exclude contractual indemnification." "Poorly written or one-sided indemnification provisions can lead to six-figure lawsuits, ruined partnerships, or even bankruptcy." — synthesized from The Lynch Law Group and ECBM. https://lynchlaw-group.com/contract-issues-for-small-business-owners-starting-with-indemnification/ ; https://blog.ecbm.com/how-an-indemnification-clause-in-a-contract-can-causes-problems-for-your-business
- "If an accident happens because of the general contractor's oversight, the subcontractor might still pay for medical bills, legal defense, and settlements." "Cases like this have bankrupted small operations overnight." — MHS Law. https://mhslaw.com/the-hidden-danger-in-your-contracts-indemnification-clauses-that-can-sink-a-small-business/

**Frequency evidence:** Weak-to-moderate. Practitioner consensus that these clauses are in "everyday" vendor, lease, partnership, and subcontractor contracts, but the sources give hypotheticals, not documented case studies or complaint counts. See gaps.

## 9. Personal guarantees (commercial leases, business loans, vendor credit)

**Plain English:** The business owner personally promises to pay if the business can't. An "unlimited" guarantee puts the owner's house, savings, and other personal assets on the line for the full remaining term of the lease or loan.

**How it burns people:** When the business fails, the landlord or lender pursues the owner personally for all remaining rent, CAM charges, taxes, late fees, and legal fees — debts that outlive the company.

**Documented harm / quote:**
- "A personal guarantee in a commercial lease makes you personally liable for rent if the business can't pay... the landlord can sue you for any unpaid rent." "An unlimited personal guarantee makes you personally liable for every dollar owed under the lease — all remaining rent, CAM charges, property taxes, legal fees, and any other obligations — for the full remaining term." — FindLaw / The Leasing Lawyers. https://www.findlaw.com/legalblogs/small-business/what-is-a-personal-guarantee-commercial-lease-and-how-to-get-out-of-one/ ; https://theleasinglawyers.com/post/personal-guarantee-commercial-lease

**Frequency evidence:** Moderate. Described as near-standard for first-time entrepreneurs and small businesses ("may be unavoidable since the company has little or no assets of its own"), but the sources are legal explainers, not complaint datasets. See gaps.

## 10. Unilateral amendment: "we may modify these terms at any time"

**Plain English:** The company reserves the right to change the contract — including price, dispute rules, and what data it can use — whenever it wants, often with notice only by posting an updated page.

**How it burns people:** Terms you agreed to can be rewritten against you without your active consent; users often don't see the change. In practice this most often smuggles in or strengthens arbitration and data-use terms.

**Documented harm / quote:**
- "Terms of service can be altered unilaterally by the firm at any time, without specific user knowledge or consent." Courts split: where "Blockbuster could change the contract unilaterally at any time, the court found that the entire contract was 'illusory' and refused to enforce the arbitration provision," while other courts uphold the change when actual notice was given. — American Bar Association, *Business Law Today*. https://www.americanbar.org/groups/business_law/resources/business-law-today/2016-may/online-contracts/ ; https://www.psh.com/online-contracts-we-may-modify-these-terms-at-any-time-right/

**Frequency evidence:** Moderate. Ubiquitous in online ToS (every major consumer platform has one); legal commentary is substantial, but I found no complaint-volume metric specific to this clause. Enforceability is genuinely unsettled in the case law.

## What I could not find / gaps

- **Hard national frequency numbers** for security-deposit disputes, indemnification disputes, personal-guarantee enforcement, termination-for-convenience invocations, and unilateral-amendment complaints. Evidence for these is practitioner commentary and "most common in small claims"-type statements, not quantified complaint datasets.
- **Liability caps / limitation-of-liability clauses** as a standalone complaint category: repeatedly mentioned alongside indemnity in freelance/SaaS guides, but I did not run a dedicated search and have no sourced complaint example. Not included as a ranked finding.
- **Fee escalators / rent-escalation clauses and late fees** in residential and commercial leases: surfaced only indirectly ("rate increases without notice" in gym-complaint summaries; CAM/escalation mentioned in commercial-lease guides). No dedicated forum/complaint source obtained — dropped rather than speculate.
- **Exclusivity, choice-of-law / jurisdiction (forum-selection) clauses**: not researched due to the search cap. Forum-selection clauses are known to appear alongside arbitration clauses but I have no direct source here.
- **Reddit / forum first-person threads**: search returned aggregator and legal-explainer pages rather than raw threads; the Contently piece is the closest to first-person freelancer accounts. Direct forum-thread sourcing is thin.
- **FTC's own pages** (consumer.ftc.gov and ftc.gov press releases) returned HTTP 403 to the fetch tool; claims attributed to the FTC here are taken from search-result summaries and secondary sources, and the GAO PDF was used as the primary citation for non-compete prevalence.
